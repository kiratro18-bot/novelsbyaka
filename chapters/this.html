A One Shot

The Hours I Sold

Nobody ruins their life in a single day.

Nobody ruins their life in a single day.

That's the lie people tell themselves.

They imagine failure as something dramatic. A car crash. A failed exam. A public humiliation. Something loud. Something obvious.

But some disasters arrive quietly.

They knock on your door every night and ask for just one more hour.

And you let them in. Again. And again. And again.

By the time you realize what happened, years are gone.

At sixteen, I thought I had unlimited time.

Everyone does. That's the privilege of being young.

You think tomorrow is guaranteed. You think you'll start next week. Next month. Next year. You think you'll become the person you're supposed to be eventually.

I believed that too.

Every night I made promises. Tomorrow I'll study harder. Tomorrow I'll start exercising. Tomorrow I'll finish that novel. Tomorrow I'll learn guitar. Tomorrow I'll talk to her.

Tomorrow. Tomorrow. Tomorrow.

The word became my favorite hiding place.

There was a girl in my class. Her name was Yuna.

She sat near the window. Whenever sunlight entered the room, it always seemed to find her first.

She laughed loudly. Talked too much. Doodled stars in her notebooks. The kind of person everyone remembered. The kind of person someone like me only watched from a distance.

I liked her for three years. Three years. And I never said a word.

Not because she was out of my league. Not because she would reject me. Because I kept waiting for the perfect version of myself. The confident version. The successful version. The version that didn't exist.

School ended. Life moved on.

I told myself there would be another chance. There always is, right? That's what people say.

One evening, years later, I saw a photo online.

Yuna. Graduation cap. Bright smile. A guy standing beside her. His arm wrapped around her shoulder. The caption was simple.

"Couldn't have done it without you ❤️"

I stared at the photo for a long time. Longer than I should have.

Not because she chose someone else. She never rejected me. The truth hurt more.

She never got the chance. Because I never tried.

That night I couldn't sleep. I opened my desk drawer looking for a distraction.

Instead, I found an old notebook. My handwriting covered the first page.

A list. "Things I want to do before I'm eighteen."

There were twenty-three goals. Twenty-three. I completed four.

I sat on the floor holding that notebook while the room grew dark around me.

  • Learn guitar
  • Write a novel
  • Get stronger
  • Make new friends
  • Travel
  • Start a business
  • Learn another language
  • Talk to Yuna

Each goal felt like a ghost. A version of me that could have existed. A future I abandoned one small decision at a time.

Then something fell from the notebook. A folded piece of paper. I opened it.

Inside was a letter. Written by thirteen-year-old me.

A letter to the future

"Hey future me."

"You're probably awesome now."

"I bet you've done a lot of cool stuff."

"Did we become a writer?"

"Did we get fit?"

"Did we travel?"

"Did we finally get brave enough to talk to the girl we like?"

"Anyway, don't give up."

"I'm counting on you."

I read it three times. Then a fourth. Then a fifth.

Because I couldn't accept it. That kid trusted me. He believed in me. He thought I was going to become someone.

And I failed him. Not all at once. A little every day. One excuse at a time. One wasted evening at a time. One tomorrow at a time.

For years I blamed temptation. Lust. Comfort. Distractions. Anything but myself.

But sitting there in the dark, I finally understood something.

Those weren't the real problem. The real problem was that I kept running. Whenever life became difficult, I escaped. Whenever I felt lonely, I escaped. Whenever I felt afraid, I escaped.

I wasn't chasing pleasure. I was avoiding reality.

And reality kept waiting for me outside my door. Growing larger every year.

A week later I visited my parents. My father was cleaning the garage. He looked older than I remembered. I helped him move boxes in silence.

After a while he asked, "So."

I looked up. "So what?"

He smiled. "What do you want to do with your life?"

The question should have been easy. I couldn't answer. Not immediately. Not honestly.

For a moment he studied me. Then nodded. Not angry. Not disappointed. Just tired.

"You still have time, you know."

That sentence followed me home. Because it wasn't criticism. It was mercy. And somehow mercy hurts more.

That night I stood in front of the mirror. Really looked at myself. Not the face. Not the body. The person. The choices. The years. The excuses. Everything.

And for the first time, I stopped asking:

"Why am I like this?"

Instead I asked:

"How many years am I willing to lose before I change?"

The next morning wasn't magical. There was no dramatic transformation. No motivational soundtrack. No sudden confidence. I was still me.

But I made my bed. Went for a walk. Read ten pages of a book. Wrote a single paragraph.

Small things. Pathetic things. Things nobody would celebrate.

But they were real. And reality, I learned, is built from small things.

Months passed. Then a year. Then two. Slowly the fog lifted. Not completely. Maybe it never does. But enough.

One evening I found that notebook again. The same one. The same list. The same dreams. This time there were check marks beside many of them. Not all. But enough to make thirteen-year-old me smile. I think.

Sometimes I still think about Yuna. Not because I love her. Not anymore. I think about what she represents. The chance I never took. The story I never started. The life I never attempted.

People think regret comes from failure. They're wrong.

Failure teaches. Failure hurts. Failure ends.

Regret is different. Regret stays. It sits beside you at night and whispers:

"What if?" "What if you had tried?" "What if you had started sooner?" "What if?"

I still hear those questions. Maybe I always will.

But now they don't make me stop. They make me move.

Because I finally learned something that took me years to understand.

You cannot get back the hours you've sold. You cannot rewrite yesterday. You cannot become the person you could have been.

But you can still become someone.

And sometimes... that's enough.

— The End —

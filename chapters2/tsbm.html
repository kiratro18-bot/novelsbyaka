The Seat Beside Me

a short story

The first thing I noticed about her was that she was late.

Not dramatically late.

Not the kind of late that makes an entire classroom turn around.

She slipped through the door three minutes after the bell, apologized to the teacher with a smile, and walked straight toward the only empty seat in the room.

The seat beside me.

“Is this taken?”

I looked at the empty chair.

“No.”

“Great.”

She dropped her bag onto it.

I stared at her.

She stared back.

Then she smiled.

“I'm Yuna.”

“I didn't ask.”

“I know.”

She pulled out her notebook.

“But you looked like you were going to.”

I went back to my book.

She laughed quietly.

That was how it started.

Yuna was annoying.

At least, that's what I told myself.

She talked too much.

She asked questions I didn't want to answer.

She borrowed my notes without asking.

She once drew a tiny cat in the corner of my mathematics worksheet and wrote:

You look like someone who needs a cat.

I crossed it out.

The next day, there were three cats.

I crossed those out too.

The day after that, she drew a whole family.

I stopped fighting.

Somehow, she became part of my routine.

The sound of her chair scraping against the floor.

Her tapping pencil.

Her quiet humming during independent work.

The way she'd whisper, “Hey,” whenever she wanted my attention.

And every morning, before I realized it, I'd look toward the classroom door.

Waiting.

I hated that.

One afternoon, she asked me to stay after school.

“Why?”

“Because I said so.”

“That's not a reason.”

“Fine. Because I'm hungry.”

“You're always hungry.”

“Exactly. You understand me.”

We went to a small convenience store near the station.

She bought two ice creams.

“You're paying.”

“Why?”

“Because you look richer.”

I looked at my uniform.

“You've never seen my bank account.”

“You have smart-person energy.”

“That's not how money works.”

“Still paying?”

I sighed.

“Yes.”

She smiled.

“See? We're becoming friends.”

“We're not.”

“Sure.”

She took the ice cream from my hand.

“Thanks, friend.”

I didn't correct her.

After that, we started walking home together.

Sometimes we talked.

Sometimes she talked and I listened.

Sometimes we walked for ten minutes without saying anything.

Those were my favorite days.

Yuna seemed to like silence too, although she never admitted it.

One evening, we sat on a bench near the river.

The sky was turning orange.

She leaned back and closed her eyes.

“Pretty, isn't it?”

“It's just a sunset.”

She opened one eye.

“You really don't know how to enjoy anything.”

“I enjoy things.”

“Name three.”

“Books.”

“That's one.”

“Sleep.”

“Two.”

“Being alone.”

She frowned.

“That's depressing.”

“It's peaceful.”

She smiled.

“Maybe.”

For a while, neither of us spoke.

Then she said quietly,

“I like places where nobody expects anything from me.”

I looked at her.

She was still smiling.

But somehow, it didn't look like her usual smile.

I wanted to ask why.

I didn't.

The first strange thing happened a few weeks later.

Yuna didn't come to school.

I didn't think much of it.

People get sick.

The next day, she returned.

She looked completely normal.

“Morning.”

“Where were you?”

She froze for half a second.

Then smiled.

“Family stuff.”

“What kind?”

“Boring stuff.”

“You're lying.”

Her smile disappeared.

Only for a moment.

Then it returned.

“You're scary when you're observant.”

“Answer me.”

“I'm fine.”

I didn't like that answer.

Not because it was vague.

Because she said it too quickly.

After that, I started noticing things.

She sometimes disappeared during lunch.

She carried a small medicine pouch inside her bag.

She avoided talking about the future.

Whenever someone asked what she wanted to do after graduation, she'd laugh and say,

“Who knows?”

Once, I heard her coughing in an empty hallway.

When I turned the corner, she was already smiling.

“Were you following me?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She walked past me.

I looked at her bag.

Then at her.

Something was wrong.

I just didn't know what.

I found out on a rainy Thursday.

I had gone back to school because I'd forgotten my phone.

The building was almost empty.

As I passed the nurse's office, I heard her voice.

“I know.”

I stopped.

“I know, Mom.”

A pause.

“I'll be careful.”

Another pause.

“No. Please don't tell them.”

Her voice became quieter.

“Especially him.”

I froze.

I didn't know why.

Maybe because I already knew who him was.

I stepped away before she could come out.

I waited outside in the rain.

She found me five minutes later.

She stared at me.

I stared back.

“You heard.”

“Yes.”

Her smile slowly disappeared.

“How much?”

“Enough.”

She looked down.

For the first time since I'd met her, Yuna didn't know what to say.

So I asked the only question I could think of.

“How long?”

She didn't answer.

That was answer enough.

We sat beneath the school entrance while the rain came down around us.

“You weren't supposed to know.”

“You weren't supposed to lie.”

“I wasn't lying.”

“You said you were fine.”

“I am fine.”

“You're dying.”

She looked at me.

Then, somehow, she smiled.

“See?”

“What?”

“You finally said it.”

I clenched my hands.

“You knew.”

“Of course I knew.”

“And you were just going to keep pretending?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

She looked toward the rain.

“Because everyone changes when they know.”

“They stop talking normally.”

“They become careful.”

“They look at you differently.”

Her fingers tightened around her sleeve.

“I don't want to become the girl everyone feels sorry for.”

I looked at her.

“You're still Yuna.”

She smiled faintly.

“That's what I wanted you to say.”

After that day, nothing really changed.

And somehow, everything changed.

We still went to the convenience store.

She still stole my food.

I still complained.

She still drew cats on my notes.

Except now, I noticed every little thing.

Every laugh.

Every stupid joke.

Every walk home.

Every time she said my name.

I started taking pictures.

She hated it.

“Delete that!”

“No.”

“My hair looks terrible.”

“It always does.”

“Rude.”

She tried to grab my phone.

I moved it away.

She laughed.

I took another picture.

That one was blurry.

It was perfect.

One evening, she gave me a small envelope.

“Don't open it yet.”

“What is it?”

“A secret.”

“I hate secrets.”

“I know.”

She smiled.

“That's why I'm giving you one.”

I put it in my bag.

“Can I open it tomorrow?”

She nodded.

“Tomorrow.”

The next morning, her seat was empty.

I waited.

Five minutes.

Ten.

The teacher entered.

“Everyone, take your seats.”

I looked at the empty chair beside me.

I kept expecting the door to open.

It didn't.

Classes passed.

Lunch came.

The chair remained empty.

At the end of the day, I finally understood.

Some empty spaces don't stay empty because someone is late.

Sometimes they're empty because someone isn't coming back.

I took the envelope from my bag.

My hands were shaking.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

Her handwriting.

You know what's funny?

When I first sat beside you, you looked like you wanted me to disappear.

I thought you hated me.

Then you started waiting for me every morning.

I noticed.

I noticed everything.

There was a small pause between the lines.

Then:

Thank you for never treating me like I was already gone.

I stopped reading.

For some reason, I couldn't continue.

But there was one final sentence.

And if you ever miss me, don't look at the empty seat.

Look outside.

I looked toward the window.

The sky was orange.

Just like that evening by the river.

I smiled.

For the first time, I understood why she always liked sunsets.

Not because they were beautiful.

Because they ended quietly.

Without asking anyone to be sad.

The bell rang.

Everyone began leaving.

I stayed where I was.

Looking at the seat beside me.

The seat that had once been nothing.

The seat that had become my favorite place in the world.

And for the first time since she left,

I didn't feel like waiting for her to return.

I simply whispered,

“See you tomorrow, Yuna.”

Then I picked up my bag.

And went home.
